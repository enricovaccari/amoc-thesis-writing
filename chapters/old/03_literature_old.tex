\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature}

This chapter reviews the literature on which the thesis is built, organised as a single argument rather than an inventory. Six strands lead to a seventh section that names the gap the thesis occupies. The first three define the problem: the psychology of climate risk perception, the cognitive science of dynamic systems, and the empirical evaluation of climate visualisation. The next two supply the response: the traditions of embodied cognition and immersive representation, and the use of machine learning as a representational rather than a predictive tool. The sixth strand is the contribution this chapter makes to the thesis itself: a design grammar that turns the preceding theory into operational principles for building an experiential representation. The final section states the intersection at which the thesis positions itself.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Psychological Barriers to Climate Risk Perception}
\label{sec:psych-barriers}

For much of the late twentieth century, climate communication rested on the \emph{information-deficit hypothesis}: the assumption that public inaction reflected a shortage of accurate information, and that closing the knowledge gap would close the action gap. Two decades of evidence have not been kind to it. \citet{kellstedt2008} found, in a representative American survey, that respondents with higher climate knowledge reported \emph{lower} personal concern and \emph{lower} sense of responsibility, an inverse relationship that survived controls for political affiliation and demographics. \citet{kahan2015} extended the point: scientific literacy interacted with cultural worldview to \emph{polarise} rather than converge climate attitudes, so that the most literate members of opposing groups disagreed most sharply. The lesson is not that information does not matter, but that its effects are mediated by cognitive and cultural machinery the standard formats were never designed to address. What has failed is not the messenger's accuracy but the messenger's grasp of who is listening.

The most influential account of this failure remains \citet{stoknes2015}, whose five psychological barriers --- the \emph{five~Ds} --- have structured much of the applied work that followed. They are \emph{distance} (climate change feels spatially, temporally and socially remote), \emph{doom} (catastrophist framing triggers avoidance), \emph{dissonance} (the gap between belief and behaviour is resolved by adjusting the belief), \emph{denial} (self-protective disengagement) and \emph{identity} (climate positions mark group belonging). The barriers interlock rather than act singly \citep{vanderlinden2015}, and the \emph{Six~Americas} segmentation of \citet{leiserowitz2021} shows that audiences stratify into psychologically coherent groups whose barriers activate differently, so that no single strategy serves all of them. The tempting inference --- that better graphs will fix this --- is not supported by the evidence the next section reviews.

The reason conventional visualisation has not dissolved these barriers is structural, not aesthetic. \citet{cornerclimate2015} showed that the imagery most used to communicate climate change --- polar bears, smokestacks, calving glaciers, temperature curves --- either reinforces the psychological distance communicators hope to close or triggers the doom response that suppresses engagement. \citet{nicholsoncole2005}, in a foundational study of how climate visuals are received, found a paradoxical mix of concern and detachment: the imagery made the phenomenon feel real but also finished, already someone else's problem. The review of \citet{ojala2023} synthesises a decade of subsequent work and concludes that formats which give the reader no active role consistently underperform formats that do. Adding precision to a line graph does not open such a role; the graph's implicit rhetoric of \emph{here is the fact} leaves the reader nowhere to stand.

The more recent literature points toward representations that treat the reader as an active participant in making meaning rather than the passive recipient of an authoritative image \citep{marlon2019, ojala2023}. This has taken several forms: narrative communication that embeds climate information in stories \citep{morris2019}, visualisation that invites exploration rather than mere reading \citep{cornerclimate2015}, and, at the experimental end, representation grounded in embodied and interactive cognition, developed in §\ref{sec:embodied-viz}. The psychological literature names the problem; the case made here is that machine learning, treated as a substrate for representation rather than a predictive instrument, offers one path to an answer.

\section{The Cognitive Science of Dynamic and Complex Systems}
\label{sec:cognitive-science}

The previous section identified the cultural and affective filters through which climate information passes. This section identifies a barrier prior to those filters and independent of them: the structural limits of the human mind in reasoning about systems governed by accumulation, feedback and delay. These limits are not gaps in education that better teaching could close. They are stable features of cognitive architecture, and any representation that ignores them will fail however well it is tuned psychologically.

The strongest evidence comes from \citet{sterman2008}, whose two decades of experiments show that even mathematically literate adults --- MIT graduate students, engineers, participants trained in calculus --- make systematic errors on elementary stock-and-flow problems. The design is simple. Participants see a scenario in which atmospheric CO\textsubscript{2} rises and stabilises at 400~ppmv by 2100 (Figure~\ref{fig:sterman-scenario}), together with the historical emissions trajectory and the current rate of removal (Figure~\ref{fig:sterman-task}). Asked to sketch the emissions path consistent with stabilisation, 84\% violate mass balance: rather than reasoning about the difference between inflow and outflow, they match the shape of the output to the shape of the input (Figure~\ref{fig:sterman-response}).

\citet{cronin2009} showed the finding in its starkest form. Participants shown the inflow and outflow of a stock were asked to sketch the stock itself --- visually integrating the difference between two curves. Even in trivial scenarios, most confused rates with levels, mistook peaks in the flow for peaks in the stock, and treated the dynamic system as static. The pattern appeared among participants who had scored highly on prior mathematics tests and, in principle, had the apparatus to solve it. This is not a failure of arithmetic but of representation: given only a graph, the mind does not spontaneously build the dynamic scenario the graph depicts. The implication is direct: presenting CO\textsubscript{2} emissions and concentration as parallel curves does not communicate that the second is the integral of the first, and readers will underestimate the system's inertia \citep{sterman2008}.

\input{figures/sterman_figures}

A converging line comes from cognitive-load theory \citep{sweller1988, swellervanmerrienboer2019}. Working memory, where active reasoning happens, has strictly limited capacity, and any format that requires holding several relationships at once produces errors that scale with their number. A static visualisation of a dynamic system --- an ensemble spaghetti plot, stacked time series, an isocontour map --- asks the reader to mentally animate what the image does not animate, to hold transitions the image does not show, to simulate a process the graph depicts only in its final state. That load is imposed \emph{on top of} the load of the domain itself. The resulting failures are not deficiencies of the reader; they are predictable consequences of asking working memory to do what it cannot. \citet{ware2021} argues that a communicative visualisation must minimise this internal-simulation demand by making the system's dynamic structure perceptually available in the representation itself.

A third strand points ahead. \citet{tversky2019}, synthesising four decades of work, argues that human cognition of complex processes is fundamentally spatial: abstract relationships are understood through analogy to bodily, spatial and kinaesthetic schemas the mind already inhabits. This is more than a fact about mental imagery; it names the substrate through which any representation of a complex system must be received to be understood at all. If systems like the AMOC are structurally dynamic, and human reasoning about dynamics is spatially embodied, then the strategies most likely to succeed are those that enrol the reader's spatial and kinaesthetic cognition. That proposition, developed next, organises the rest of the chapter.

\section{Empirical Evaluation of Climate Visualisation}
\label{sec:empirical-viz}

The previous sections established the psychological filters and the cognitive limits that shape what any representation can achieve. This section turns to what has actually been measured about the reception of climate visualisations, and where the evidence is thin. The field is methodologically asymmetric: rich in studies of what audiences \emph{prefer}, sparse in studies of what they \emph{understand}. That asymmetry, more than any deficit of aesthetics or interactivity, defines the gap this thesis addresses.

The most systematic mapping is \citet{harold2016}, whose review set the climate-visualisation literature against what perceptual science knows about non-expert processing. Their finding: the standard formats --- line graphs of global mean temperature, choropleth maps of projected change, ensemble spaghetti plots, tornado diagrams --- are mismatched to the cognitive resources of their audiences, and the mismatch appears not only among students but in the interpretive errors of trained policy readers. \citet{mcmahon2015} corroborated this, showing that policy actors reading IPCC visualisations systematically underestimate uncertainty ranges shown as shaded envelopes and misread ensemble spread as disagreement rather than as a structural feature of the projection. \citet{retchless2016}, testing alternative encodings of climate uncertainty on maps, found that the encodings most common in scientific practice --- bivariate colour schemes, stippled overlays --- produced the worst comprehension among non-specialists, while encodings borrowed from cartographic design performed measurably better. The point is not that one encoding wins, but that alternatives exist and can be evaluated.

The more troubling structural finding is the gap \citet{kause2020} identify between preference and comprehension. In one of the few studies to measure both in the same participants, the visualisations people \emph{preferred} --- rated clearest, most engaging, most trustworthy --- were not those that produced the highest \emph{comprehension}. People preferred what felt familiar; they understood what was structurally aligned with the phenomenon, whether or not they preferred it. A later extension \citep{kause2021} found the same split for uncertainty: intuitive icon arrays and confidence bars produced overconfident readings, while less intuitive but structurally faithful frequency formats produced calibrated ones. The consequence is rarely stated plainly: preference-based evaluation, the dominant tradition, measures the wrong variable. Optimising for preference risks producing what \citet{sheppard2015} calls \emph{consoling visualisations} --- images received warmly and understood poorly.

\subsection{The perceptual basis of visual encoding}
\label{subsec:encoding}

The finding that some encodings communicate better than others is not accidental; it rests on a mature science of visual encoding that the design work of Chapter~\ref{ch:translate} draws on directly. The founding grammar is Bertin's: any mark encodes data through a finite set of \emph{visual variables} --- position, size, value, colour, orientation, texture \citep{bertin1983}. These variables are not interchangeable. \citet{clevelandmcgill1984}, in the field's foundational experiment, ranked them by decoding accuracy: people judge position along a common scale far more accurately than length, angle, area or --- near the bottom --- colour saturation. \citet{munzner2014} turned this ranking into a design rule, \emph{effectiveness}: match the most important information to the most accurately decoded channel, and do not spend a precise channel on a secondary variable. \citet{ware2021} grounds the same rule in perception, distinguishing channels processed pre-attentively --- registered in parallel, before focused attention --- from those that demand serial inspection. Together these establish that encoding is a matter of measured perceptual performance, not taste, and that a representation's choices can be defended or faulted on evidence. This is the backbone of the design grammar assembled in §\ref{sec:design-grammar}.

\subsection{The special problem of uncertainty}
\label{subsec:uncertainty}

Uncertainty is where conventional encoding fails most sharply, and where this thesis departs most from convention. The standard devices --- error bars, shaded envelopes, confidence bands --- are read poorly by non-specialists, who treat the band as a hard edge or as disagreement rather than as probability \citep{mcmahon2015, spiegelhalter2017}. \citet{padilla2018} explain why with a cognitive framework: static uncertainty encodings force a mental computation that overloads working memory, so viewers fall back on heuristics that discard the uncertainty. The best-evidenced alternative is to \emph{animate} it. Hypothetical Outcome Plots show a sequence of possible outcomes over time, letting the viewer feel the spread instead of decoding a band \citep{hullman2015hops}; untrained observers judge them more accurately than error bars or ensembles \citep{kale2019hops}. This licenses the central design move of the thesis --- rendering uncertainty as a felt perceptual quality rather than a bracketed number --- and grounds it in evidence rather than intuition \citep{padillakay2022}.

\subsection{The existing AMOC visual baseline}
\label{subsec:amoc-baseline}

The comparison at the centre of this thesis requires a control condition, and that control must reflect how the AMOC is \emph{actually} communicated, not an invented straw figure. Reviewing the authoritative sources establishes what that baseline is. In the scientific and public record, the AMOC is presented almost entirely through a small set of conventional forms: line graphs of transport strength in Sverdrup over time, model-ensemble projections of twenty-first-century decline shown as a bundle of scenario curves with a shaded spread, and reconstructed multidecadal ``fingerprint'' indices derived from sea-surface temperature \citep{caesar2018, ipcc2021}. Institutional assessment adds a further layer: the IPCC communicates the AMOC's future through \emph{verbal confidence qualifiers} --- ``very likely to weaken'', ``medium confidence'' that no abrupt collapse occurs before 2100 --- attached to these curves \citep{ipcc2021}.

Each of these forms carries real information, and each is limited in exactly the ways the earlier sections predict. The static transport curve depicts a dynamic system in its final state, leaving the reader to mentally animate a process the cognitive-science literature shows they cannot \citep{sterman2008, cronin2009}. The ensemble spread encodes uncertainty as a shaded band, the encoding \citet{mcmahon2015} and \citet{padilla2018} show is systematically misread. The verbal confidence qualifiers compound the problem: they ask a non-specialist to hold a probabilistic judgement about a threshold in working memory, with no perceptual support at all. And none of these forms conveys the non-linearity that defines a tipping element --- the possibility that a gradual weakening could reorganise abruptly into a different regime. The conventional baseline, in short, communicates the AMOC's magnitude and trend while failing to communicate its dynamics, its non-linearity and its uncertainty. This is precisely the target the experiential condition is designed to improve upon, and reconstructing the baseline faithfully from these published forms is what makes the comparison in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluate} a fair one.

\section{Embodied Cognition, Data Humanism and Immersive Analytics}
\label{sec:embodied-viz}

The first three sections defined the problem. This section turns to the traditions in which a response has taken shape. Three related lines of work --- embodied cognition, data physicalisation and humanism, and immersive analytics --- converge on a proposition that runs against mainstream scientific visualisation: that the intelligibility of complex information depends on the reader's active bodily and perceptual engagement, and that representations which do not enrol the body and senses underperform those that do. Each tradition reaches this from a different direction, and together they scaffold the design work of Chapter~\ref{ch:translate}.

The philosophical grounding is \citet{lakoff1999}: the abstract concepts through which humans reason about complex systems are not disembodied logical structures but extensions of bodily and spatial experience. \emph{Acceleration}, \emph{feedback}, \emph{stability}, \emph{tipping} are understood through kinaesthetic schemas the mind already inhabits --- a body speeding up, a movement returning to itself, a footing that holds, a footing that gives way. \citet{kirsh2013} supplied experimental support: participants allowed to gesture and move while reasoning about complex processes outperformed those restricted to static inspection, across mathematical, mechanical and dynamical problems. The consistency of the effect has since grounded a design literature arguing that representations of complex systems should be treated not as pictures to view but as environments to inhabit \citep{tversky2019, hurtienne2011}. The implication is not that graphs should become simulations, but that any representation whose reader must reason about dynamic behaviour should offer some embodied engagement --- kinaesthetic, exploratory or perceptual --- that inert formats do not.

The most concrete design tradition to grow from this is \emph{data physicalisation}, defined by \citet{jansen2015} as the construction of physical artefacts whose geometry and material encode information for exploration through touch, sight and sound. \citet{moere2010}, in an early comparison, found that participants working with physicalisations recalled the represented information more accurately and retained it longer than those working with equivalent bar charts, a result since extended across data types. Parallel to it, the \emph{data humanism} of \citet{lupi2017} and colleagues argues that data communication has been dominated by an aesthetic of dispassionate abstraction that treats the reader as a rational processor rather than an embodied being. The data-humanist alternative --- hand-drawn, narrative, foregrounding authorial voice --- has met mixed reactions, but its central empirical claim is stronger than its surface suggests: studies of narrative visualisation \citep{segel2010} and of visual explanation grounded in personal experience \citep{morris2019} consistently document deeper engagement from representations that acknowledge the reader as a participant.

The third tradition, more recent, is \emph{immersive analytics} \citep{marriott2018}: the premise that immersive display --- virtual and augmented reality, large-format and spatially-aware interfaces --- offers representational affordances the flat screen cannot match, and that these suit data whose structure is spatial, dynamic or multidimensional. Immersive analytics is oriented to \emph{analysis by exploration}: rather than presenting a finished view, it invites the analyst to move through the data, change viewpoint, select and compare, leveraging the embodied resources Lakoff and Kirsh identify \citep{chandler2015}. Formal evaluations remain limited, and the strongest current evidence concerns three-dimensional spatial and temporal tasks rather than abstract complex systems. But the theoretical alignment with the cognitive science of §\ref{sec:cognitive-science} is direct, and the enabling technologies --- browser-based WebGL for rendering, the Web Audio API for real-time sonification --- now bring immersive-analytic design within reach of a single research project. This thesis takes a deliberate step into that space, treating the browser as a medium for embodied representation rather than a delivery channel for visualisations that could as easily be printed.

Taken together, these traditions support a claim that structures the rest of the thesis: the difficulty of communicating complex climate systems is not addressable by refinement within the conventional-visualisation paradigm, and the direction in which the empirical, cognitive and design literatures collectively point treats the reader as an active, embodied participant in constructing understanding. The design of the representational conditions in Chapter~\ref{ch:translate} is grounded in this claim, and defended through the commitments this section has articulated.

\section{Machine Learning as a Representational Substrate}
\label{sec:ml-climate}

The preceding sections established the problem and the design traditions in which a response takes shape. This section turns to the fifth strand: machine learning for climate science, which supplies the analytical substrate on which the representational work of Chapter~\ref{ch:translate} is built. The field is now vast and surveyed elsewhere \citep{reichstein2019, sonnewald2021, irrgang2021}; the purpose here is to identify its dominant agenda and to locate, against it, the alternative role this thesis takes up.

That agenda is overwhelmingly \emph{predictive}. The most cited applications fall into four families: short-range forecasting of climate variables through recurrent and convolutional networks \citep{sonnewald2021, weyn2020}, anomaly detection in observational streams \citep{ham2019}, subgrid parameterisation of physical processes in numerical models \citep{rasp2018, brenowitz2020} and statistical downscaling of coarse model output \citep{vandal2019}. The AMOC-specific literature mirrors this: work applying machine learning to detect early-warning signals in reconstructed transport records \citep{boers2021, ditlevsen2023, benyami2024}, to forecast variability from surface indicators \citep{lu2024} and to identify latent dynamical modes in reanalyses \citep{sonnewald2019}. The field has produced results of real value and settled a set of conventions. The point is not that this agenda is misconceived, but that it is oriented, almost without exception, toward new findings \emph{for expert audiences}, and its very success has left an adjacent question unexplored.

That question is what machine learning contributes when the goal is not a new result but to make an existing scientific object \emph{intelligible to non-specialists}. The distinction is easy to lose, so state it precisely. A network trained to forecast AMOC transport six months ahead produces a new capability, evaluated against a held-out record in shared units --- correlation, root-mean-square error, calibration. A dimensionality-reduction analysis of the same record produces a latent representation whose value cannot be judged in those terms, because it answers a different question --- one about \emph{structure}: what few coordinates account for most of the record's variability, and what do they mean physically? The analytical work of Chapter~\ref{ch:data_pipeline} is of the second kind, and the design work of Chapter~\ref{ch:translate} rests on it. The claim is not that the AMOC's vertical manifold has a novel structure --- the finding is consistent with decades of oceanographic understanding \citep{hannachi2007, buckley2016} --- but that this structure, once recovered, can serve as the coordinate system through which the AMOC is made available to non-specialist perception. Here machine learning is not predictive but \emph{representational}: it produces a substrate for representation, not a forecast whose accuracy is the object of evaluation.

The literature treating machine learning in this representational role is thin but not empty. \citet{iten2020}, in fundamental physics, argued that latent representations recovered by unsupervised networks can be treated as legitimate epistemological objects --- ways a computational system \emph{proposes} a variable a human can then examine and name. \citet{camps2019} extended a related argument to seismology, using autoencoder representations of waveforms as the basis for interactive exploration. In visualisation research, \citet{sacha2018} developed a framework for \emph{visual analytics with machine learning} in which the model's latent representation is not the final product but the substrate through which an analyst builds understanding. Extending this from expert analytics to public communication has been proposed occasionally \citep{munzner2014, cabrera2023} but implemented in the climate context almost nowhere. The specific proposition of this thesis --- that latent representations recovered from an invisible climate system can be translated into experiential representations legible to non-specialists --- is coherent with the embodied-cognition traditions of §\ref{sec:embodied-viz} and the emerging visual-analytics tradition here, but its systematic empirical development in climate communication has not been attempted. The gap is defined at that intersection.

\section{Toward a Design Grammar for Experiential Representation}
\label{sec:design-grammar}

The preceding sections explain why conventional visualisation fails and why an embodied, experiential response is worth attempting. They do not yet say \emph{how} such a representation should be built. This section closes that gap. It distils, from the literature already reviewed, a small set of design principles that connect a property of the system to a perceptual channel through a documented cognitive reason. The aim is to ensure that the choices made in Chapter~\ref{ch:translate} are derivations, not decorations: each traceable to a source, each defensible in isolation.

The foundation is a single principle from which the others follow. Bertin identified the finite set of visual variables through which any mark encodes data \citep{bertin1983}. \citet{clevelandmcgill1984} established, by experiment, that these variables are not equal: position along a common scale is decoded far more accurately than colour, area or angle. \citet{munzner2014} turned this into a design rule --- \emph{effectiveness}: assign the most important information to the most accurately decoded channel, and never waste a precise channel on a secondary variable \citep{ware2021}. This is the parent principle. Everything below is its application to the properties of a complex climate system.

The first application concerns \emph{magnitude}. A system's dominant quantity --- for the AMOC, the strength of the overturning --- is a continuous, ordered value with a natural strong-to-weak polarity. By the effectiveness rule it belongs on spatial position, the most accurate channel, not on colour saturation, which sits near the bottom of the hierarchy \citep{clevelandmcgill1984, tversky2019}. The second concerns \emph{dynamics}: since people cannot mentally animate a static frame into a process \citep{sterman2008, cronin2009}, change over time should be carried by actual motion, which the visual system tracks pre-attentively, not left for the viewer to reconstruct \citep{ware2021}. The third concerns \emph{anomaly}: a departure from the normal regime is, perceptually, a departure from visual regularity, well carried by local changes in density or texture that draw bottom-up attention without a legend \citep{bertin1983, ware2021}.

The fourth application is where the thesis departs most from convention: \emph{uncertainty}. The standard devices are read poorly by non-specialists, who treat a band as a hard edge or as disagreement rather than as probability \citep{mcmahon2015, padilla2018}. Padilla's framework explains why: static uncertainty encodings force a mental computation that overloads working memory \citep{padilla2018}. The best-evidenced alternative is to \emph{animate} uncertainty --- to show a sequence of possible outcomes over time, so the viewer feels the spread instead of decoding it, as Hypothetical Outcome Plots do \citep{hullman2015hops, kale2019hops}. This licenses rendering uncertainty as a felt perceptual quality --- variability in motion, density or sound --- rather than a bracketed number. A fifth channel, \emph{sound}, extends the same logic beyond vision: continuous auditory properties such as pitch, rhythm and timbre can carry ordered data in parallel with the visual channel, adding capacity without visual clutter \citep{hermann2011}.

These principles are collected in Table~\ref{tab:design-grammar}. Stated generally, they compose a transferable grammar: a mapping from \emph{system property} to \emph{perceptual channel}, justified by a \emph{cognitive principle} and anchored to a \emph{source}. Chapter~\ref{ch:translate} applies this grammar to the features the analysis of Chapter~\ref{ch:data_pipeline} recovers from the AMOC. The grammar is stated generally on purpose: its value is not confined to one ocean current but extends to any invisible system whose structure must be made perceptible --- which is what makes it a contribution rather than a set of local choices.

This grammar also locates the thesis against existing practice. A tradition of experiential data art already treats the latent space of machine-learning models as perceptual material to be inhabited: the studio fuse*, for instance, navigates the latent space of generative networks as a terrain of morphing form.\footnote{fuse*, \emph{Artificial Botany} and related works, \url{https://fuseworks.it}. The studio describes its interest as the ``intermediate dimension'' of the latent space, explored for expressive fluidity rather than for communicating a measured system.} This work is visually powerful and shares the thesis's core intuition --- that a learned latent structure can become something to perceive, not only to compute. What it does not do is test whether an audience \emph{understands} anything as a result; it is experience without measured comprehension. The contribution of this thesis is exactly the step that tradition omits: to ground each perceptual choice in the principles of Table~\ref{tab:design-grammar}, and to test, comparatively, whether the resulting representation supports understanding. The grammar is what separates a defensible representation from a merely beautiful one.

\section{Synthesis: The Intersection This Thesis Occupies}
\label{sec:literature-synthesis}

Read as one argument, the strands reviewed here converge on a proposition no single strand states alone. The psychological literature (§\ref{sec:psych-barriers}) shows that the reception of climate information is filtered through documented barriers that conventional formats fail to address and sometimes reinforce. The cognitive-science literature (§\ref{sec:cognitive-science}) shows these barriers are compounded by structural limits on reasoning about accumulation, feedback and delay from static images. The empirical literature (§\ref{sec:empirical-viz}) shows that climate visualisation has been evaluated mostly for preference rather than comprehension, that a mature science of perceptual encoding exists to ground better choices, and that uncertainty is where conventional encoding fails most sharply. The embodied-cognition and immersive literatures (§\ref{sec:embodied-viz}) identify the design traditions in which a response is taking shape, but their application to climate communication has been sporadic and rarely evaluated. The machine-learning literature (§\ref{sec:ml-climate}) has advanced rapidly but predictively, leaving the representational role of latent structure almost unexplored in the climate context. And the design grammar (§\ref{sec:design-grammar}) shows that these strands can be combined into operational principles --- but that no existing work has done so and tested the result.

The intersection where these meet is narrow and empirically unpopulated. It is defined by five commitments: to a climate system whose invisibility and non-linearity make it a stress test for the representational problem \citep{lenton2008, armstrongmckay2022}; to a machine-learning treatment that recovers latent structure rather than forecasts outcomes \citep{sonnewald2019, iten2020}; to a design tradition that treats the reader as an embodied, active participant \citep{lakoff1999, jansen2015, marriott2018}; to a comparative framework in which conventional and experiential formats are evaluated against one another \citep{harold2016, kause2020}; and to a qualitative, exploratory evaluation appropriate to a phenomenon not yet ripe for population-level inference \citep{braunclarke2006, smithmcgannon2018}. No study reviewed here satisfies all five jointly, and the few that satisfy three or four are scattered across the disciplinary literatures rather than integrated in one programme. The gap is not the absence of any one commitment; it is the absence of their integration.

The thesis's response is deliberately modest in scope and specific in claim. It applies machine learning to the observational and reanalysis records of a single system, the AMOC, to recover a latent structure whose faithfulness is defensible on the oceanographic literature's own terms \citep{hannachi2007, buckley2016}. It designs a conventional static condition and a dynamic, experiential one, grounding every design decision in the principles of §\ref{sec:design-grammar}. It evaluates the two through a small, exploratory, qualitative-primary study whose ambition is interpretive depth, not population-level generalisation. The contribution is not a novel finding in any one of these literatures but a methodology that connects them --- and the case for its value is the argument this chapter has now made. The chapters that follow are its execution.
