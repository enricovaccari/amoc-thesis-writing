\section{The Problem of Knowing What We Cannot See}
\label{sec:problem-of-knowing}

The systems that most powerfully shape the twenty-first century are, with few exceptions, invisible. The slow acidification of the ocean, the silent collapse of insect populations, the redistribution of heat through the atmosphere, the subterranean dynamics of the cryosphere: these are processes that unfold across timescales longer than careers and spatial scales larger than continents. They are measured by instruments that the public does not see, recorded as numbers the public does not read, and projected by models the public is asked to trust without ever being shown how those models think. The defining cognitive condition of the present is not, as is sometimes claimed, a shortage of information. It is a chronic mismatch between the structure of the systems that govern our future and the structure of the minds that must reckon with them.

The mismatch is not incidental. It is well documented. \citet{stoknes2015} identifies five psychological barriers that systematically blunt the public's engagement with climate information --- distance, doom, dissonance, denial and identity --- and shows that the conventional formats of climate communication (reports, line charts, projection maps) tend to reinforce rather than soften these barriers. \citet{vanderlinden2015} have shown empirically that climate risk is perceived as psychologically distant on three axes simultaneously --- spatial, temporal and social --- and that this triple distance reduces both cognitive engagement and willingness to act. \citet{spence2012} found the same pattern in a representative survey of the British population: increasing the salience of psychological proximity raised concern; the same information presented at a remove did not.

The deeper problem is structural, and it predates the climate debate. \citet{sterman2008}, in a body of work spanning two decades, has shown that even mathematically literate adults struggle systematically to reason about systems with stocks and flows, with delays, with feedback. When asked to predict the trajectory of a quantity governed by simple accumulation, educated participants make errors that betray a deep cognitive tendency to confuse rates with levels, inflows with stocks, and to treat dynamic systems as if they were static --- as if a graph of CO\textsubscript{2} emissions were a graph of CO\textsubscript{2} concentration, which it is not. The error is not one of arithmetic; it is one of representation. The mind has not been given a form in which the dynamic structure of the system can be held. And what cannot be held cannot be acted on.

This thesis takes as its starting point the proposition that the public understanding of climate risk is not an educational problem to be solved by more information, nor a communication problem to be solved by better wording. It is a \textit{representational} problem: a problem of finding forms in which the dynamic, multi-scale, often non-linear structure of climate systems can be perceived and inhabited, not merely read.

\section{The Gap: Why Information Does Not Translate to Understanding}
\label{sec:the-gap}

The empirical literature on climate visualisation has matured considerably over the past decade, and its central finding is sobering. \citet{harold2016} reviewed the state of climate-data visualisation against what is known from cognitive and perceptual science about how non-experts process visual information. Their conclusion is that the formats most commonly used to communicate climate data --- line graphs of global mean temperature, choropleth maps of projected change, ensemble spaghetti plots --- are mismatched to the cognitive resources of the audiences they are meant to inform. They assume a comfort with abstraction, a familiarity with uncertainty representation, and an internal capacity to mentally animate static frames into dynamic processes that the general public does not, in fact, possess.

Three difficulties compound. The first is the difficulty of \textit{dynamic systems}: as \citet{sterman2008} has shown, static visualisations of inherently dynamic processes systematically mislead, because the reader sees a curve where the system is a flow. The second is the difficulty of \textit{non-linearity}: tipping elements --- thresholds beyond which a system reorganises into a different stable regime --- violate the linear intuition that small causes produce small effects, and the visualisation conventions inherited from the natural sciences are poorly equipped to convey the discontinuity that defines them \citep{lenton2008, armstrongmckay2022}. The third is the difficulty of \textit{uncertainty}: the error bar, the shaded envelope, the ensemble spread --- the standard graphical conventions for communicating uncertainty --- are processed by non-expert readers in ways that often invert their intended meaning, treating uncertainty as evidence of disagreement or unreliability rather than as the structural feature of a forecast that it actually is \citep{spiegelhalter2017}.

The result is a paradox the climate communication community has long recognised but not resolved. The quantity of climate information available to the public has never been greater, and the scientific consensus on the main contours of climate change has never been firmer. Yet the public's grasp of the \textit{behaviour} of the climate system --- its non-linearity, its inertia, its capacity for abrupt reorganisation --- has not kept pace. The information has been delivered. The understanding has not.

This gap is the territory of this thesis. The argument advanced here is that closing it will require not more information, nor better-worded information, but information presented in \textit{different representational forms}: forms that exploit the perceptual and embodied resources of the human reader, forms in which the structure of the system is encoded into the structure of the experience. The claim is not that experiential representation is a universal solution; it is that it is an under-explored region of the representational space that deserves rigorous, comparative empirical study. Whether it works --- and under what conditions --- is the question this thesis sets out to address.

To investigate whether experiential representations of complex climate systems can improve public understanding, the thesis takes a single system as its case study: the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, hereafter the AMOC.

\section{Thesis Statement}
\label{sec:thesis-statement}

This thesis investigates how machine-learning--derived representations of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation can be designed to improve the understanding of complex climate tipping risk among non-specialist audiences. It proposes that the challenge of communicating systems like the AMOC is not predictive but \textit{representational}: even where the data are available, the system remains cognitively inaccessible. It addresses this challenge by combining machine-learning analysis of ocean transport dynamics with interactive and experiential visualisation, and by evaluating the resulting representations through a small-scale qualitative user study. The contribution offered is not a new oceanographic finding, but an empirical bridge: a transferable methodology for translating the latent structure of an invisible system into a form that humans can perceive, and for evaluating, with appropriate epistemic modesty, whether that translation works.
