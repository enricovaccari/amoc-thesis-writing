\section{The Problem of Knowing What We Cannot See}
\label{sec:problem-of-knowing}

The systems that most powerfully shape the twenty-first century are, with few exceptions, invisible or hard to fully grasp, even by experts. The slow acidification of the ocean, the silent collapse of insect populations, the redistribution of heat through the atmosphere, the subterranean dynamics of the cryosphere: these are processes that unfold across timescales longer than careers and spatial scales larger than continents. They are measured by instruments that the public does not see, recorded as numbers the public does not read, and projected by models the public is asked to trust without ever being shown how those models think. The defining cognitive condition of the present is not, as is sometimes claimed, a shortage of information. It is a chronic mismatch between the structure of the systems that govern our future and the structure of the minds that must reckon with them.

The mismatch is not incidental. It is well documented. \citet{stoknes2015} identifies five psychological barriers that systematically blunt the public's engagement with climate information --- distance, doom, dissonance, denial and identity --- and shows that the conventional formats of climate communication (reports, line charts, projection maps) tend to reinforce rather than soften these barriers. Stoknes works in the tradition of cognitive psychology applied to climate risk, a field that itself traces back to \citet{kahnemantversky1979} and their work on heuristic biases in decision-making under risk. The core idea
is that the human brain is not wired to process slow, distant, probabilistic risks the same way it processes an immediate, concrete danger. This is precisely why your work does not stop at analyzing RAPID data: if science alone were enough to produce understanding, the gap \citet{stoknes2015} describes would not exist.\footnote{The main outcome of this thesis, named \textit{Living Atlantic}, is a concrete attempt to
work around that bias, not by explaining more but by making the scale and
urgency of the phenomenon felt through a perceptual channel different from
the purely analytical one.} \citet{vanderlinden2015} have shown empirically that climate risk is perceived as psychologically distant on three axes simultaneously --- spatial, temporal and social --- and that this triple distance reduces both cognitive engagement and willingness to act. \citet{spence2012} found the same pattern in a representative survey of the British population: increasing the salience of psychological proximity raised concern; the same information presented at a remove did not.\footnote{The early warning signals this thesis searched for, namely rising variance and rising autocorrelation before a
critical transition \citep{scheffer2009}, are structurally the same
phenomenon as psychological distance as characterized by
\citet{spence2012}: both are weak signals, hard to notice unless you know
what to look for, that precede an important state change. In the ocean
system, the weak signal is statistical and buried in depth data. In the human psyche, the weak signal is the apathetic indifference that precedes action, or its absence.}

The deeper problem is structural, and it predates the climate debate. \citet{sterman2008}, in a body of work spanning two decades, has shown that even mathematically literate adults struggle systematically to reason about systems with stocks and flows, with delays, with feedback. When asked to predict the trajectory of a quantity governed by simple accumulation, educated participants make errors that betray a deep cognitive tendency to confuse rates with levels, inflows with stocks, and to treat dynamic systems as if they were static --- as if a graph of CO\textsubscript{2} emissions were a graph of CO\textsubscript{2} concentration, which it is not. The error is not one of arithmetic; it is one of representation. The mind has not been given a form in which the dynamic structure of the system can be held. And what cannot be held cannot be acted on.

This thesis takes as its starting point the proposition that the public understanding of climate risk is not an educational problem to be solved by more information, nor a communication problem to be solved by better wording. It is a \textit{representational} problem: a problem of finding forms in which the dynamic, multi-scale, often non-linear structure of climate systems can be perceived and inhabited, not merely read.

\section{The Gap: Why Information Does Not Directly Translate to Understanding}
\label{sec:the-gap}

The empirical literature on climate visualisation has matured considerably over the past decade, and its central finding is sobering. \citet{harold2016} reviewed the state of climate-data visualisation against what is known from cognitive and perceptual science about how non-experts process visual information. Their conclusion is that the formats most commonly used to communicate climate data --- line graphs of global mean temperature, choropleth maps of projected change, ensemble spaghetti plots --- are mismatched to the cognitive resources of the audiences they are meant to inform. They assume a comfort with abstraction, a familiarity with uncertainty representation, and an internal capacity to mentally animate static frames into dynamic processes that the general public does not, in fact, possess.

Three difficulties compound. The first is the difficulty of \textit{dynamic systems}: as \citet{sterman2008} has shown, static visualisations of inherently dynamic processes systematically mislead, because the reader sees a curve where the system is a flow. The second is the difficulty of \textit{non-linearity}: tipping elements --- thresholds beyond which a system reorganises into a different stable regime --- violate the linear intuition that small causes produce small effects, and the visualisation conventions inherited from the natural sciences are poorly equipped to convey the discontinuity that defines them \citep{lenton2008, armstrongmckay2022}. The third is the difficulty of \textit{uncertainty}: the error bar, the shaded envelope, the ensemble spread --- the standard graphical conventions for communicating uncertainty --- are processed by non-expert readers in ways that often invert their intended meaning, treating uncertainty as evidence of disagreement or unreliability rather than as the structural feature of a forecast that it actually is \citep{spiegelhalter2017}.

The result is a paradox the climate communication community has long recognised but not resolved. The quantity of climate information available to the public has never been greater, and the scientific consensus on the main contours of climate change has never been firmer. Yet the public's grasp of the \textit{behaviour} of the climate system --- its non-linearity, its inertia, its capacity for abrupt reorganisation --- has not kept pace. The information has been delivered. The understanding has not.

This gap is the territory of this thesis. The argument advanced here is that closing it will require not more information, nor better-worded information, but information presented in \textit{different representational forms}: forms that exploit the perceptual and embodied resources of the human reader, forms in which the structure of the system is encoded into the structure of the experience. The claim is not that experiential representation is a universal solution; it is that it is an under-explored region of the representational space that deserves rigorous, comparative empirical study. Whether it works --- and under what conditions --- is the question this thesis sets out to address.

\section{The Case Study: AMOC as a Stress-Test}
\label{sec:case-study-amoc}

The argument of \S\ref{sec:problem-of-knowing} and \S\ref{sec:the-gap} is general; the work of this thesis is necessarily specific. To investigate whether experiential representations of complex climate systems can improve public understanding, the thesis takes a single system as its case study: the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, hereafter the AMOC. The choice is not arbitrary. A system chosen to stress-test the representational problem posed in the preceding sections must exhibit, in extreme form, every property that makes such problems hard. The AMOC exhibits all of them at once. It is literally invisible, in the sense that no surface signature is legible to the naked eye and no everyday experience gives access to it. It is spatially distributed at a basin scale, and its behaviour cannot be inferred from any single location. It operates on multidecadal timescales that exceed human experience and, in the observational record, exceed the record itself. It is non-linear in the strong sense: a candidate tipping element whose weakening could organise itself into an alternative circulation regime with hysteresis \citep{lenton2008, armstrongmckay2022}. And it has civilisational stakes: a substantial slowdown would reorganise the heat budget of the North Atlantic and, with it, the climate of Europe \citep{buckley2016}.

The AMOC is the meridional component of the North Atlantic circulation that transports warm surface waters northward and returns colder, denser waters southward at depth, sustained by density contrasts of thermohaline origin.\footnote{The physical apparatus of the circulation --- its governing equations, the Stommel two-box model of bistability, and the derivation of meridional transport as a volume integral --- is developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}. This chapter treats the AMOC only insofar as it defines the representational problem.} Its strength is conventionally measured in Sverdrup (\SI{1}{\sverdrup} = \SI{e6}{\cubic\metre\per\second}), and since April 2004 has been observed continuously by the RAPID-MOCHA-WBTS array at \ang{26.5} north \citep{frajkawilliams2019, moat2026}. This observational record --- roughly two decades in a system with variability at multidecadal periods --- is the empirical substrate of Part~I. It is complemented by the Copernicus Marine Service GREP reanalysis ensemble, which extends the analysis back into the pre-observational period at the cost of introducing ensemble spread as an epistemic quantity in its own right.

The scientific case for treating the AMOC as a representational priority is not, however, only that it is difficult to see. It is that the community has begun to see something troubling in it, and disagrees about what. \citet{caesar2018} reconstructed a fingerprint of AMOC weakening from sea-surface temperature patterns and argued that the circulation had reached a millennial minimum. \citet{boers2021} applied early-warning-signal statistics to the reconstructed record and reported evidence consistent with a system approaching a critical transition. \citet{ditlevsen2023} extended this line of analysis and proposed a probabilistic tipping window centred on the mid-twenty-first century --- a claim that received extensive public coverage and equally extensive methodological challenge from within the field \citep{benyami2024}.\footnote{The methodological reply of \citet{benyami2024} argues that the confidence intervals in \citet{ditlevsen2023} depend on assumptions about the proxy record --- specifically the treatment of the sea-surface temperature reconstruction as a stationary indicator --- that are not adequately justified. The dispute is unresolved at the time of writing; the point relevant here is that the disagreement itself demonstrates the representational problem this thesis addresses.} The independent observational record from RAPID, meanwhile, has not shown a monotonic slowdown consistent with the reconstructed multidecadal trend \citep{rahmstorf2015, moat2026}, leaving the field with a partial and contested empirical picture. This is precisely the kind of system where representational clarity is most needed and least available: a system whose stakes are civilisational, whose evidence is contested at the level of methodology and not merely interpretation, and whose mechanism is invisible to direct observation.

\section{Greenland as Cognitive Bridge: From the Visible to the Invisible}
\label{sec:greenland-bridge}

The AMOC is the system this thesis addresses, but it is not the system the general public sees. When non-specialists are asked to evoke an image of climate change, they rarely nominate a subsurface circulation; they nominate ice \citep{oneill2014}. The Greenland Ice Sheet is iconic in the specific technical sense used in visual communication studies --- it has a documented recurrence in journalism, satellite imagery and public discourse that no oceanic feature can rival. It is one of the small handful of images through which the climate system reaches non-expert cognition at all. The system that is scientifically most consequential, in short, is not the system that is culturally most legible; and the mismatch is not incidental to the argument of this thesis, it is the ground on which the argument is built.

The two systems are, however, physically coupled. Freshwater discharge from the accelerating mass loss of the Greenland Ice Sheet enters the subpolar North Atlantic and reduces surface density; a lower-density surface layer inhibits the deep convective mixing that renews North Atlantic Deep Water; and a weakened deep-water formation weakens the meridional pump that sustains the AMOC \citep{boning2016, rahmstorf2015, caesar2018}.\footnote{The mechanism is well established in principle but quantitatively uncertain in current attribution studies. Direct RAPID observations at \ang{26} north do not yet resolve the freshwater signal cleanly against interannual variability, and the extent to which the multidecadal reconstructed weakening is attributable to Greenland forcing --- rather than to internal variability or to anthropogenic aerosol forcing --- remains an active research question \citep{caesar2018}.} What the public sees --- ice retreating from the terminus of a glacier --- is causally connected to what the public does not see: a slowing engine of ocean heat transport that regulates the climate of an entire continent.

This thesis exploits that coupling narratively as well as physically. Greenland enters not as a second case study, whose analytical treatment would double the scope of the work, but as the cognitive entry point through which the invisible mechanism can be approached. It is the piece of the story the reader arrives holding; the argument then leads that reader from the seen to the inferred, from the symptom to the system. This trajectory --- from ice to ocean, from what a satellite photograph makes obvious to what only a decade of moored current-meter observations at a single latitude can suggest --- is the narrative structure of the experiential prototype developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:translate}, and it is the pedagogical logic that runs beneath every representational decision the thesis will defend.

\section{On the Interdisciplinary Nature of the Work}
\label{sec:mission}

The work presented here sits at an intersection that reflects the author's disciplinary orientation: the meeting point of physical science, computational methods and the perceptual conditions under which non-specialists encounter complex systems. The commitment that runs beneath every methodological choice in the thesis is that these three domains are not adjacent but continuous --- that the credibility of a scientific claim, the machinery through which the claim is derived and the form in which it becomes intelligible to a non-specialist reader are not separable problems, and treating them as such is one of the reasons the public understanding of climate risk has been so difficult to advance.

Machine learning is discussed here neither as a predictive technology nor as an automation tool, but in a third and less familiar role: as a \emph{representational substrate} for systems that cannot be observed directly. The proposition is that the latent geometries recovered by unsupervised methods --- what a compressed representation preserves and what it discards --- can be treated as legitimate objects of design, becoming the coordinate system through which a system too complex for direct intuition is made available to perception. Recent work in the physical sciences has begun to argue for a similar epistemological role for learned representations \citep{iten2020}, though its extension into public communication is, to the author's knowledge, largely unattempted. The mission of the thesis is to test that extension in one specific, well-bounded case, with the methodological discipline required to make what is learned transferable beyond it.

\section{Purpose, Relevance and Alignment with the Sustainable Development Goals}
\label{sec:purpose}

The relevance of the work proposed here operates at three levels, which the thesis will attempt to substantiate without overclaiming any of them. 

The first is academic. The field concerned with the public communication of complex climate systems --- what has come to be called \emph{complexity communication} --- is methodologically thin. The empirical literature is dominated by preference studies, in which participants are shown alternative visualisations and asked which they find clearer or more engaging \citep{harold2016}. Comprehension itself, tested against ground-truth understanding of system behaviour, is rarely the object of measurement, and experiential formats --- interactive, embodied or sonically encoded --- are almost absent from the evaluated evidence base. This thesis contributes not another visualisation nor another survey, but a comparative methodology in which the same underlying data are presented in three representational conditions of increasing experiential richness and evaluated qualitatively for the conceptual understanding they support. To the author's knowledge, this methodology has not previously been applied to the AMOC or to any system of comparable structural complexity.

The second level of relevance is institutional, and here the alignment with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals is genuine rather than nominal. The primary anchor is \textbf{SDG~13, Climate Action}, and specifically target~13.3, which commits signatories to ``\textit{improve education, awareness-raising and human and institutional capacity on climate change mitigation, adaptation, impact reduction and early warning}'' \citep{un2015}. Every noun in that sentence --- education, awareness, capacity, early warning --- names a task the thesis is engaged with. A secondary anchor is \textbf{SDG~4, Quality Education}, insofar as the work concerns the pedagogical problem of how conceptually difficult content is transmitted to non-specialists; and a further, more indirect anchor is \textbf{SDG~14, Life Below Water}, given that the AMOC is a component of the ocean processes on which marine and coastal systems depend. The alignment claim is that the thesis contributes concrete methodological work to the awareness-raising and capacity-building objectives that these goals name.

The third level is practical, and it concerns the transferability of the methodology beyond its case study. The AMOC is one of an identified family of climate tipping elements --- the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, the Amazon rainforest, boreal permafrost, the Indian summer monsoon, the coral reef systems --- each of which shares the structural features that make the representational problem hard: invisibility to direct perception, multidecadal to centennial dynamics, non-linear response with hysteresis, and civilisational stakes \citep{lenton2008, armstrongmckay2022}. The framework developed here --- machine learning as analytical substrate, experiential representation as communicative output, qualitative evaluation as feedback loop --- is designed to be portable. The AMOC is the stress-test on which the framework is validated; it is not the endpoint at which the framework is exhausted. If the argument of the thesis holds, its methodology will be usable, with appropriate domain adaptation, on any climate system whose scientific gravity exceeds its cultural legibility.

The three levels reinforce each other. The academic gap justifies the methodological work; the institutional alignment justifies the choice of case study; and the practical transferability justifies the investment of thesis-scale effort in a single system. Before a society can act on a system, it must first be able to picture it, and this thesis is one contribution to the work of learning how.

\section{Thesis Statement}
\label{sec:thesis-statement}

This thesis investigates how machine-learning–derived representations of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation can be designed to improve the understanding of complex climate tipping risk among non-specialist audiences. It proposes that the challenge of communicating systems like the AMOC is not predictive but \emph{representational}: even where the data are available, the system remains cognitively inaccessible. It addresses this challenge by combining machine-learning analysis of ocean transport dynamics with interactive and experiential visualisation, and by evaluating the resulting representations through a small-scale qualitative user study. The contribution offered is not a new oceanographic finding, but an empirical bridge: a transferable methodology for translating the latent structure of an invisible system into a form humans can perceive, and for evaluating, with appropriate epistemic modesty, whether such translation supports understanding. The methodological commitment underlying every choice in the thesis is that machine learning here serves as a \emph{substrate}, not as the deliverable --- the deliverable is intelligibility.

\section{Scope, Boundaries and Honest Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

The thesis pursues its argument within deliberate boundaries, and the honest declaration of those boundaries is itself part of the methodological discipline the work claims to embody. Four commitments deserve to be made explicit at the outset.

First, this is not a thesis in oceanography. The primitive equations of ocean circulation \citep{vallis2017}, the numerical schemes through which they are solved, and the physical mechanisms through which the AMOC responds to external forcing are treated as inputs, not as objects of contribution. Where these mechanisms are discussed --- in Chapter~\ref{ch:background} for the theoretical apparatus, and in Chapter~\ref{ch:detect} for the analytical pipeline --- the discussion is at the level of what a well-informed non-oceanographer needs in order to understand the representational problem, not at the level at which the oceanographic community itself debates it.

Second, this is not a thesis in forecasting. A machine-learning approach to the AMOC that would be tempting for its scientific ambition --- a recurrent architecture trained to produce short-range probabilistic projections of transport strength with calibrated uncertainty bands --- has been considered and explicitly deferred. It is scoped as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}. The reason for the deferral is not a limitation of the observational record or of computational resources; it is a decision about the argument the thesis is trying to make. Adding a predictive layer would risk shifting the argumentative centre of gravity from representation --- where the thesis claims a contribution --- to prediction, where it would be adding, at best, an incremental result to a well-populated literature \citep{sonnewald2021}.

Third, the empirical study through which the representational framework is evaluated is qualitative and exploratory by design. It engages a small number of participants (eight to twelve) in think-aloud protocols with the three representational conditions, and analyses the resulting transcripts through thematic analysis in the tradition of \citet{braunclarke2006}. The thesis does not claim, and its methodology cannot support, statistically powered inference to a wider population. What it claims is what such a study can legitimately claim: an initial mapping of the ways in which non-specialists articulate their understanding of an invisible system under different representational conditions, with the qualitative depth appropriate to a first empirical investigation of an under-studied intervention.

Fourth, the experiential prototype produced in Chapter~\ref{ch:translate} is a research artefact, not a product. It is designed to be legible to a small number of participants under controlled conditions, not to be deployed to an unbounded public. This distinction matters because a production artefact would require different design choices --- around robustness, accessibility, cultural translatability and long-term maintenance --- that lie outside the scope of a Master's thesis. The prototype is the vehicle through which the thesis's design claims are made testable; extending it beyond that role would be a research programme of its own.

The pattern in these four boundaries is not defensive. Each of them is a decision, taken consciously, to keep the centre of gravity of the thesis on the specific contribution it is claiming: a methodology for translating an invisible system into an experientially available representation and for evaluating that translation with epistemically honest tools. Scope-freeze is treated here as a methodological principle, not as an apology, and the supervisor's guidance during the development of this work has been that excess of scope, not insufficiency, is the risk against which a thesis of this ambition most needs to be defended.

\section{Roadmap}
\label{sec:roadmap}

The thesis is organised in three parts, each addressing one stage of the detect–translate–evaluate arc that structures the argument.

\textbf{Part~I --- Foundations} establishes the problem this thesis addresses and the case study through which it is investigated. Chapter~\ref{ch:rqs} formalises the research questions, objectives and hypotheses that structure the work. Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} reviews the relevant literature across five domains --- psychological barriers to climate risk perception, the cognitive science of dynamic systems, empirical evaluation of climate visualisation, embodied and immersive representation, and machine-learning applications to climate science --- and identifies the intersection at which this thesis positions itself. Chapter~\ref{ch:background} develops the physical and mathematical apparatus needed to treat the AMOC as a measurable and analysable object.

\textbf{Part~II --- Translation} documents the constructive work of the thesis. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} specifies the three-stage methodology --- analytical, design-based and empirical --- through which the argument is made testable. Chapter~\ref{ch:detect} presents the analytical pipeline: the dimensionality-reduction, anomaly-detection and early-warning analyses through which machine learning recovers the latent structure of the observational record, and reports the results that motivate the representational choices that follow. Chapter~\ref{ch:translate} designs and documents the three representational conditions --- conventional static, interactive dynamic and machine-learning-driven experiential --- through which that latent structure is translated for non-specialist audiences.

\textbf{Part~III --- Evaluation and Reflection} closes the argumentative loop. Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluate} specifies the qualitative user study through which the three conditions are compared. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports its findings. Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} interprets those findings in conversation with the literature reviewed in Part~I and with the analytical results of Part~II, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes with reflections on limitations, transferability to other invisible climate systems and future work.
